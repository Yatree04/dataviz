The Line
That Moves

A Pacific coastline is traced by satellite every year. Over twenty-five years of imagery, it does not sit still. This is an attempt to follow the mechanism from a tonne of carbon to a moving shoreline — and to be honest about where the chain holds and where it does not.

The input

It begins with heat they barely added

Every nation in this story contributes a negligible share of global emissions. But emissions do not stay where they are made, and atmospheric heat is not a local phenomenon. That asymmetry is the premise of everything below.

The chart is on a logarithmic axis, because the usable series span three orders of magnitude and a linear axis renders the small emitters as invisible slivers — which would have made the headline above unreadable.

⚠ Two data problems, flagged rather than explained. Palau's series reads 86.7 t per capita — but it starts at 190.6 t in 1970 and falls roughly 60% since, while tourism grew. No industrial or tourism footprint produces that shape; it is a denominator or bunker-fuel allocation artifact. Nauru, Guam, the Marshall Islands, N. Mariana Is. and American Samoa sit at a flat 0.1 for fifty consecutive years, which is missing data wearing a number. Those bars are hatched and excluded from the range annotation.

The heat store

The ocean took the heat — about 91% of it

When greenhouse gases trap outgoing radiation, the energy has to accumulate somewhere. The IPCC's assessment of the Earth's energy budget puts roughly 0% of it in the ocean. The remainder goes into land, ice and atmosphere. That is why an atmospheric problem becomes an oceanic one — and why the rest of this chain runs through seawater.

Sea surface temperature anomaly (°C vs 1971–2000 mean)

Two things this chart deliberately shows

The shaded ribbon is uncertainty. The SPC export ships a standard error alongside every observation. Its median is 0.1 °C — and the values themselves are rounded to 0.1 °C. Much of the year-to-year detail sits inside that band, so the ribbon is drawn rather than footnoted, and the tooltip prints one decimal instead of pretending to two.

The shaded columns are ENSO. El Niño and La Niña are the dominant mode of Pacific interannual variability. Nearly every large wiggle in this line is an ENSO event, not a step in the emissions trend. Naming them is the difference between showing a trend and letting the reader mistake noise for one. They recur in the sea level and rainfall charts below, because the same oscillation drives those too.

Why there is no "land vs sea" comparison here

An earlier version of this page plotted land surface temperature against sea surface temperature and captioned it "the ocean accumulates heat more slowly." That comparison does not survive contact with the data. In this export the two series correlate at r = 0.96–0.99 with a mean absolute difference of 0.03–0.10 °C — smaller than the 0.1 °C rounding step. For Fiji they are literally the identical number in 61% of years; for Tuvalu, 67%.

The reason is structural: both come from the same 5° × 5° NOAAGlobalTemp field, over grid cells that are almost entirely ocean. There is no independent land station network on an atoll. Globally land does warm faster than ocean — but this dataset cannot show that, so the claim has been removed rather than illustrated with two copies of the same line.

First consequence

Warm water is bigger water

Heat does not only melt ice. When seawater warms it expands, so the same mass occupies more volume. This thermal expansion accounts for roughly 40% of sea-level rise across the twentieth century, and closer to half of the rise since 1971.

The Pacific has a clear upward trend. The absolute numbers are small in metres — but for an island nation whose highest natural point is a few metres above sea level, the relevant question is not the mean, it is how often the extreme now reaches further inland.

The Copernicus source is centimetre-resolution — re-exporting from .Stat with more decimals would remove this caveat entirely.

Sea level anomaly (m vs 1993–2012) — Pacific regional mean

The dips are not pauses

The 1998 minimum in this series is the 1997–98 El Niño; the 2020–22 high is the triple-dip La Niña. Western Pacific sea level is strongly modulated by trade-wind strength, which is why regional rates ran two to three times the global mean through the 1990s and 2000s before moderating. A regional trend is not a global trend, and the SPC metadata says so explicitly.

Second consequence

The reef did not empty. It flattened.

Coral is an animal — a colony of polyps living with photosynthetic algae inside their tissue. When water stays above a local seasonal maximum for long enough, the coral expels those algae, turns white, and begins to starve. That is bleaching, and the Pacific has been through four global events since 1998.

What happened next is the part most climate narratives get wrong, including an earlier draft of this one. Across 50 datasets and 15,482 surveys, the GCRMN found that Pacific mean hard coral cover stayed broadly stable at about 25.5% from 1990 to 2022 — falling sharply during the 1998 and 2014–2017 events, then recovering within about six years.

The damage is real, but it is not a cover statistic. Composition shifted. Branching Acroporidae and Pocilloporidae declined while massive Poritidae held steady. The reef kept its area and lost its architecture — and architecture, not cover, is what breaks waves.

Marked bands are the four observed global mass bleaching events. There is no threshold line here on purpose — see below.

Why there is no "+1 °C bleaching threshold" line

An earlier version drew a dashed line at +1 °C and labelled it a bleaching-stress threshold. That was wrong in kind, not just in placement. Bleaching stress is Degree Heating Weeks accumulated above a site's Maximum Monthly Mean — a local, seasonal-peak quantity. This chart shows an annual mean anomaly against a 1971–2000 baseline. An annual mean averages away exactly the summer peak that kills coral, so no horizontal line on this axis can represent a bleaching threshold.

The old line also only rendered when the maximum of the land or sea series exceeded 1.0, so it silently disappeared for 13 of 22 countries — including Tuvalu, the Marshall Islands, Palau and Samoa — while the caption kept referring to it. For New Caledonia and Nauru it was triggered by the land series, which has nothing to do with coral.

Observed event years are used instead. They are recorded fact rather than a threshold inferred from the wrong axis.

What the GCRMN surveys actually found

GCRMN, Status and Trends of Coral Reefs of the Pacific: 1980–2023 (DOI 10.59387/WIUJ2936). Sea surface temperature over Pacific reefs rose 0.82 °C between 1985 and 2022 — about 0.22 °C per decade.

A parallel signal

Rain that no longer keeps to its season

Rainfall is shifting too, but not in one direction. Some islands trend drier, some wetter. Neither is safe when catchment, agriculture and water storage were all built around a pattern.

The bars are ten-year means, not single years — because "tends drier" is a claim about a tendency and one year of an anomaly series is weather. The hollow dot is the most recent single year, kept visible so you can see how far one year sits from the tendency.

Rainfall anomaly (mm) — ten-year mean by country

Read the gap between the dot and the bar

The most recent year shows a sharp dry anomaly through the central equatorial Pacific — Nauru, Kiribati, Tuvalu — and wet conditions in the southwest. That dipole is the classic ENSO rainfall signature, not a greenhouse trend. It is the single clearest illustration on this page of why one year cannot carry a climate claim, which is why both quantities are drawn together.

The mechanism

So the waves arrive less broken

Water has risen. The reef has kept its cover and lost its structure. These are two consequences of the same heat, and they act on the same thing: how much wave energy reaches the shore.

Coral reefs dissipate on average 0% of incoming wave energy, with the reef crest alone accounting for about 86% of that. The dissipation is frictional — it depends on hydraulic roughness, which is built by branching, plating, three-dimensional growth forms. Precisely the forms that declined.

Modelling a reef in Moorea, Carlot and colleagues found that if structural complexity is halved, the wave run-up height that currently occurs once in 100 years becomes 0× more frequent.

That is the hinge: higher water plus a flatter reef means the extreme arrives more often, even if the average does not change much. The shore is exposed to a frequency it was not built around.

Ferrario et al. 2014, Nature Communications (97% average dissipation) · Carlot et al. 2023, Scientific Reports (Moorea, 1-in-100-year run-up)

⚠ The limit of this link. Carlot et al. is a hydrodynamic model of one site in French Polynesia, and the 50× figure applies to a specific return period, not to waves generally. Nothing on this page demonstrates that structural complexity has halved anywhere in the Pacific — the GCRMN data establishes the direction of the compositional shift, not its magnitude in complexity units. This is the mechanism, stated as a mechanism.

Which matters only because of who is standing there

The missing middle

The hazard grew. So did the exposure.

A hazard only becomes a risk where people are. This is the part of the chain that is easiest to skip and hardest to argue with: while the reef flattened and the water rose, more people moved to within walking distance of both.

0% growth in population living within 5 km of a Pacific reef, since 2000
0 cyclones passing within 100 km of a Pacific reef, 1980–2023
+0 sea surface warming over Pacific reef areas, 1985–2022 (≈0.22 °C/decade)

Two of those three are hazard. The first is exposure — and it is the one that turns a physical chain into a human one. A reef that dissipates less energy in front of a shore where nobody lives is an ecological story. In front of a shore where a third more people now live, it is a risk story.

All three figures: GCRMN, Status and Trends of Coral Reefs of the Pacific 1980–2023. These are published regional headline values, transcribed and cited — not computed from a dataset downloaded for this project. For a per-country exposure layer, SPC publishes coastal population at 1, 5 and 10 km from the coast; that is the natural next build.

The measurement

You don't have to model this — but you do have to read it carefully

Every point on this map is a measured rate of shoreline change in metres per year, from satellite data across the Pacific. Red retreats. Blue builds. Click any point for its rate.

Notice how much blue there is. That is not a flaw in the data, and this piece would be dishonest if it hurried past it.

Where the simple version of this story breaks

Reef islands are not passive platforms being overtopped. They are sediment landforms, continuously built from material the reef produces. A reanalysis of 709 islands across 30 Pacific and Indian Ocean atolls found that no atoll lost land area and 88.6% of islands were stable or grew. In Tuvalu — where local sea level rose at roughly twice the global rate — total land area increased by 2.9% over four decades, with eight of nine atolls gaining.

So "sea level rises, therefore islands shrink" is not what the measurements say, and a submission that claimed it would be corrected within a minute by anyone who works on Pacific coasts.

What the measurements do say is that shorelines are highly mobile. Islands migrate across their reef platforms, gain on one side and lose on the other, and reorganise after storms. Habitability is threatened less by area loss than by three things this mobility does not capture: episodic overwash reaching further inland, salinisation of the thin freshwater lens beneath the island, and degradation of the reef that supplies the sediment in the first place. That last one is where this chain actually closes.

Where the chain is felt

The line is where a home was

Cyclone Winston made landfall in Fiji on 20 February 2016 — the most intense tropical cyclone recorded in the Southern Hemisphere at landfall.

0 people were directly affected.

Winston is not offered here as proof of the cascade. It is what the cascade is about: the gap between a shoreline that moves a few metres a year and a shoreline that moves in one afternoon. The mechanisms in this piece govern how far that afternoon reaches inland, and how often it comes.

⚠ Attributing a single storm to warming is a hard scientific claim and nothing on this page demonstrates it. Winston also formed during the 2015–16 El Niño, the same event marked on the charts above. What is defensible: warmer seas raise the intensity ceiling available to cyclones, a flatter reef dissipates less of the resulting wave energy, and higher mean water lets surge start from a higher point. Those are three mechanisms, not an attribution.

Corrections and known limits — what changed in this revision, and why
  1. Coral cover. An earlier version claimed the Pacific had lost ~50% of living coral cover since the 1980s and cited the GCRMN Pacific report for it. That report finds cover broadly stable at ~25.5% (1990–2022). The claim was removed and the beat rebuilt around the finding the report does support: a compositional shift away from branching forms, reducing structural complexity.
  2. Temperature baseline. Axes previously read "vs 1850–1900". The SPC metadata specifies 1971–2000. Corrected on every chart and stated inside the chart so it cannot drift from the copy.
  3. Bleaching threshold. A "+1 °C bleaching-stress threshold" was drawn on an annual mean anomaly. Bleaching stress is Degree Heating Weeks above a site's Maximum Monthly Mean — an annual mean cannot express it. The line rendered for only 9 of 22 countries and was sometimes triggered by the land series. Replaced with observed global bleaching events.
  4. Land vs sea comparison. Removed. The two series correlate at r = 0.96–0.99 with a mean difference below the data's own rounding step; they are effectively the same line.
  5. Uncertainty. The ERROR_VAL / ERROR_TYPE columns are now loaded and drawn as a ±1 standard-error ribbon. Tooltips print one decimal, matching the 0.1 °C resolution of the data.
  6. ENSO. Added as an annotation layer across the temperature, sea level and rainfall beats. It is the dominant driver of Pacific interannual variability and was previously invisible, which invited readers to attribute it to the emissions trend.
  7. Emissions scale. Moved to a logarithmic axis so the sub-4 t cluster is legible. Palau, Nauru, Guam, the Marshall Islands, N. Mariana Is. and American Samoa are flagged as export artifacts rather than explained as footprints.
  8. Rainfall. Changed from a single-year snapshot to a ten-year mean, because the surrounding copy makes trend claims. The single year is retained as a dot to show spread.
  9. Coastline framing. Rewritten to engage the atoll geomorphology literature (Duvat 2019; Kench et al. 2018) rather than assert simple retreat, which the measurements do not support.
  10. Exposure. Added as its own beat. The stated arc is hazard → exposure → impact and the middle term was previously absent.

Still open. Sea level is quantised to 0.1 m in this export and should be re-fetched at source resolution. The exposure beat uses published regional headline figures rather than a per-country dataset.
