The Line That Moves

Satellites have measured the same Pacific coastlines every year since 1999. The lines have moved. This is an account of the physics that moved them — and of the one measure of coastal defence that nobody in the Pacific is currently monitoring.

Scroll ↓
01

A coastline, measured twenty-five times

Digital Earth Pacific traces Pacific shorelines from Landsat imagery once a year. The result is not a model or a projection. It is twenty-five separate measurements of where the water met the land, and they do not sit on top of each other.

Drag to move through the annual shoreline positions, 1999–2023. Digital Earth Pacific, Landsat annual shorelines.

02

The forcing was not produced here

Eleven of the seventeen Pacific series in the SPC emissions file sit below 4 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per person per year. Kiribati is at 0.9, the Solomon Islands at 0.8, Tuvalu at 0.4. New Caledonia, at 18.4, is an outlier built on nickel smelting rather than on how anyone lives.

Six series in the same file are not usable. Palau is recorded at 190.6 tonnes per person in 1970 and 86.7 in 2024 — figures four times higher than any national total ever recorded anywhere. Nauru, the Marshall Islands, Guam, American Samoa and the Northern Marianas are recorded as flat lines at 0.1–0.2 tonnes for fifty-five consecutive years, which is not a measurement either. Those six are drawn hatched below and excluded from the map.

Six of seventeen national series in GHG_EMI_CAPITA fail a plausibility check and are excluded from the choropleth. They are shown hatched rather than hidden, because a gap you can see is more honest than a country that quietly vanishes.

03

All ten of the warmest years on record are this century

The SPC sea-surface temperature file runs from 1850 to 2025 for twenty-one countries and territories. Averaged across all of them, the ten warmest years in that 176-year record are 2024, 2025, 2020, 2017, 2016, 2019, 2023, 2018, 2015 and 2022. There is no twentieth-century year in the list.

The regional mean sat at −0.12 °C through the 1961–1990 climatological baseline. Across 1995–2024 it sits at +0.37 °C. Against the 1850–1900 pre-industrial window the last ten years average +0.74 °C. The ocean absorbs roughly 91% of the excess energy in the climate system (IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 9).

The sawtooth on top of the trend is ENSO. El Niño years lift regional sea-surface temperature by several tenths of a degree and La Niña years pull it back; 2016 and 2024 are both El Niño peaks. Any Pacific series read without that band shading will look noisier than it is.

04a

A regional signal built from unusable national ones

The SPC sea-level file reports at 0.1-metre resolution. Every observation in it is one of five values: −0.2, −0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. At that precision no single country's series means anything — Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, not because the sea stood still.

Averaged across all twenty-one territories, the quantisation error cancels. The regional mean rises at 4.3 mm per year across 1993–2023, which is within the range satellite altimetry independently reports for the tropical Pacific. The dataset cannot answer which island is worst affected. It can answer whether the Pacific is rising, and it says yes.

Individual country sea-level series are not plotted here, at any zoom level, because 0.1 m resolution cannot support a country comparison. Only the 21-territory mean is shown.

04b

The islands with no rivers are the ones drying out

Rainfall anomaly is not moving in one direction across the Pacific. Fitting a linear trend to each of the twenty-two national series in RAIN_ANOM (1979–2025) splits the region almost evenly: eight countries drying, fourteen wetting.

The split is not random. The four fastest-drying territories are Nauru (−4.9 mm per decade), New Caledonia (−4.0), Kiribati (−2.3) and Tuvalu (−2.0). Three of those four are low atoll states with no rivers, no lakes and no highland catchment. They hold fresh water in a thin lens floating on seawater beneath the sand, recharged by rain and by nothing else.

Tonga (+5.2 mm per decade), Micronesia (+4.4), Niue (+4.3) and the Solomon Islands (+4.1) are getting wetter. Neither direction is neutral. Infrastructure, cropping calendars and catchment sizing were built for the distribution that used to hold.

Nauru's year-to-year standard deviation is 30.7 mm — nearly three times New Caledonia's 14.3 mm on a far smaller land area. The trend is downward and the variance around it is the largest in the region.

06

The reef held. That is not the same as the defence holding.

The obvious version of this story is that Pacific coral is dying and the coasts are losing their breakwater. The data does not support it. GCRMN's 2025 assessment, drawing on more than 15,000 surveys at over 8,000 sites between 1987 and 2023, found average Pacific hard coral cover stable at about 25.5% from 1990 to 2022 — a result it attributes to the region's size, its ecological diversity and its comparatively small coastal population.

Percent cover is the wrong measure of coastal protection, and stability in it is not reassurance.

Reefs break waves by being rough. A global meta-analysis of 255 studies found that reefs reduce incident wave energy by an average of 97%, with the reef crest alone dissipating 86% of it. The mechanism is friction against structure: branches, plates, spurs and grooves extracting energy from moving water. Carlot and colleagues measured that structure directly at Mo'orea and put the Nikuradse roughness of a healthy fore reef at about 0.42 metres, in a range of 0.34 to 0.52 — a reef surface physically rough at the scale of a forearm.

Roughness and cover are different quantities and they can move independently. Two reefs at 25% cover dissipate different amounts of wave energy if one is built of branching Acropora and the other of massive Porites colonies. Thermal stress does not only kill coral; it reshuffles which coral survives. A reef can recover its cover and not recover its architecture.

The consequence is steeply non-linear. Carlot's hydrodynamic modelling found that if the structural complexity of a healthy reef is halved, wave run-up heights that currently occur once a century arrive fifty times more often.

No reef-structure data is plotted in this beat, because none exists for the Pacific at regional scale. The roughness figures are site measurements from Mo'orea, French Polynesia; the run-up multiplier is a modelled result from the same site. They are presented as mechanism, not as a Pacific-wide measurement.

Nothing in the Pacific Data Hub measures reef roughness. Not one of the thirteen climate indicators in DF_CLIMATE_CHANGE describes reef structure, and GCRMN's monitoring is built on benthic cover because cover is what a diver with a transect line can record. The quantity that governs whether a wave reaches a village is the quantity nobody is tracking at regional scale.

What is being tracked is exposure, and it is rising. GCRMN counts 945 cyclones passing within 100 km of Pacific reefs between 1980 and 2023, and the human population living near those reefs up 28.7% since 2000. The reef held its cover. The number of people standing behind it grew by more than a quarter.

GCRMN, Status and Trends of Coral Reefs of the Pacific: 1980–2023 (doi.org/10.59387/WIUJ2936) · Ferrario et al. 2014, Nature Communications 5:3794 · Carlot et al. 2023, Scientific Reports 13:1683.

07

Where the rain stopped arriving, the water did not

The SDG 6.1.1 series tracks the share of each population using a safely managed drinking-water service. Kiribati — one of the four fastest-drying territories in the rainfall data — moved from 60.9% in 2000 to 75.7% in 2022. A quarter of the country still lacks it, and the national figure conceals the more useful one: Kiribati's urban population is 28.3 percentage points ahead of its rural population.

Papua New Guinea reports the widest gap in the region at 42.5 points, on a national coverage of 50.2%. Vanuatu is at 10.6, Fiji at 7.6. Three territories moved backwards between 2000 and 2022: French Polynesia (−10.2 points), Niue (−2.2) and Wallis and Futuna (−0.3).

Rainfall is the recharge term for an atoll's freshwater lens. It is also, in several of these countries, the only one.

SDG 6.1.1 values are WHO/UNICEF JMP modelled estimates interpolated between sparse household surveys, not annual measurements. They are shown here as 2000-versus-2022 endpoints and as urban–rural gaps — the two comparisons the estimation method supports. No annual trend line is drawn.

08

Ten million transects, no model

Digital Earth Pacific's rates-of-change layer fits a trend to each shoreline transect across the region and reports metres gained or lost per year. Ten point four million of them. Red retreats, blue builds.

Click any transect for its measured rate. Digital Earth Pacific, rates_of_change, 2023.

The Pacific pattern is not uniform retreat, and the literature says the same: Duvat's 2019 synthesis of 709 islands and Kench's 2018 work on Tuvalu both find most atoll islands stable or growing in area, as reef-derived sediment redistributes. Land area and habitability are different things. An island can gain area on its ocean side and lose the village on its lagoon side in the same decade.

09

Two and a half million people, and three quarters of them in four years

The SPC disaster file records directly affected persons attributed to disasters for twenty-one Pacific countries and territories from 2005 to 2023. The total is 2,588,223.

Three quarters of that total falls in four years. 2016 accounts for 733,906, almost all of it Fiji after Cyclone Winston. 2020 accounts for 548,988, spread across Vanuatu (246,802) and Fiji (235,921) after Harold and Yasa. 2018 accounts for 462,927, and 2015 for 195,993 after Pam. The years between them are mostly in the tens of thousands.

Fiji alone accounts for 1,240,734 affected persons across the eighteen years reported — more than the country's population. Some Fijians appear in that total more than once.

Recorded mortality across the same file is far smaller and differently distributed: Papua New Guinea 1,415, Fiji 1,100, Samoa 491, Solomon Islands 378. Pacific disaster mortality is low relative to the number affected, which is what functioning early warning looks like. Displacement, damaged housing and lost water supply are what the affected count measures, and it is rising in bursts.

VC_DSR_AFFCT is the only clean indicator in this file. The others mix units — one reports persons, another reports economic loss in USD under a similar label. Reporting completeness also varies by country and year, so a low national total may mean few events or thin reporting. Sparse-reporting countries are marked on the axis.

Conclusion

The shoreline in the first panel moved for reasons that are individually measurable and collectively unmonitored. The heat is in the record. The rise is in the record, once you average away the rounding. The rainfall shift is in the record, and it is sharpest in the places with the least freshwater storage.

The reef is in the record too, and it says the cover held.

What is not in any record is whether the reef is still rough enough to do the work — the one property that decides how much of the ocean's energy arrives at the door.

Published null result

Crop yield was tested as a downstream link from rainfall. Detrending each national yield series and correlating the residual against that country's precipitation anomaly gives coefficients between −0.15 and +0.23 across fifteen territories, with a maximum of 0.23 for Fiji. That is not a relationship worth drawing an arrow for. The Micronesian series also drops from 91,566 to 5,587 kg/ha across the record, which is a change in what is being counted rather than in what is being grown. Crop yield is therefore reported here as tested and excluded, not omitted.
