The Pacific Cascade

What a warming ocean does on its way to land. Greenhouse gas becomes heat. Heat becomes a rising sea and a rearranged rainy season. Those become a coastline that no longer sits where it did. Each of those steps has been measured. One has not and it is the step that decides how much of the ocean's energy reaches a doorway.

Eleven of the seventeen Pacific series in the SPC emissions file sit below 4 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per person per year. Kiribati is at 0.9, the Solomon Islands at 0.8, Tuvalu at 0.4. New Caledonia, at 18.4, is an outlier built on nickel smelting rather than on how anyone lives.

Six series in the same file are not usable. Palau is recorded at 190.6 tonnes per person in 1970 and 86.7 in 2024 — four times higher than any national figure recorded anywhere. Nauru, the Marshall Islands, Guam, American Samoa and the Northern Marianas appear as flat lines at 0.1–0.2 tonnes for fifty-five consecutive years, which is not a measurement either. The SPC sea-surface temperature file runs 1850 to 2025 across twenty-one countries and territories. Averaged across all of them, the ten warmest years in that 176-year record are 2024, 2025, 2020, 2017, 2016, 2019, 2023, 2018, 2015 and 2022. No twentieth-century year appears in the list.

The regional mean sat at -0.12 °C through the 1961–1990 climatological baseline. Across 1995–2024 it sits at +0.37 °C. Against the 1850–1900 pre-industrial window, the last ten years average +0.74 °C.

The sawtooth riding on the trend is ENSO. El Niño years lift regional sea-surface temperature by several tenths of a degree and La Niña years pull it back; 2016 and 2024 are both El Niño peaks. Any Pacific series read without that band shading looks noisier than it is.

The ocean absorbs roughly 91% of the excess energy in the climate system (IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 9). From here the cascade splits in two.

A regional signal built from unusable national ones

The SPC sea-level file reports at 0.1-metre resolution. Every observation in it is one of five values: -0.2, -0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. At that precision no single country's series means anything — Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, not because the sea stood still. Averaged across all twenty-one territories, the quantisation error cancels. The regional mean rises at 4.3 mm per year across 1993–2023, within the range satellite altimetry independently reports for the tropical Pacific. The dataset cannot answer which island is worst affected. It can answer whether the Pacific is rising, and it says yes.

Where the rain stopped arriving, the water did not

The SDG 6.1.1 series tracks the share of each population using a safely managed drinking-water service. Kiribati — one of the four fastest-drying territories in the rainfall data — moved from 60.9% in 2000 to 75.7% in 2022. A quarter of the country still lacks it, and the national figure conceals the more useful one: Kiribati's urban population runs 28.3 percentage points ahead of its rural population.

Papua New Guinea reports the widest gap in the region at 42.5 points, on national coverage of 50.2%. Vanuatu is at 10.6, Fiji at 7.6. Three territories moved backwards between 2000 and 2022: French Polynesia (-10.2 points), Niue (-2.2), Wallis and Futuna (-0.3).

Rainfall is the recharge term for an atoll's freshwater lens. In several of these countries it is the only one. That is the mechanism, and it is why this link is drawn dashed: the rainfall decline is measured and the water-access shortfall is measured, but nothing in either dataset attributes one to the other.

Ten million transects, no model
Digital Earth Pacific's rates of change layer fits a trend to each shoreline...

...The Pacific pattern is not uniform retreat, and the literature agrees: Duvat's 2019 synthesis of 709 islands and Kench's 2018 work on Tuvalu both find most atoll islands stable or growing in area as reef-derived sediment redistributes. Land area and habitability are different things. An island can gain area on its ocean side and lose the village on its lagoon side in the same decade.
This layer measures the outcome. It does not apportion it between the links above.

The cascade is measured everywhere except where it matters most
The heat is in the record: the ten warmest years, all within this century.
The sea anomalies are in the record, marking a Pacific that is becoming increasingly unstable.
The rise is in the record, once the noise is averaged away — 4.3 millimetres a year.
The rainfall shift is in the record, uneven and sharpest where freshwater storage is already limited.
The shoreline change is in the record, traced across millions of transects.
And the gas is in the record — accumulating upstream of every one of these changes.
Between the gas and the shoreline sits a chain of measurable shifts: gas → heat → sea anomalies → sea-level rise → rainfall → shoreline.
Every solid link in this cascade was built by someone deciding the quantity was worth counting.
The question is what happens between the measurements — and what remains invisible because we have not yet decided how to measure it.
