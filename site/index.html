What a warming ocean does on its way to land.

The Pacific Cascade

Greenhouse gas becomes heat. Heat becomes a rising sea and a rainy season that keeps moving. Those become a coastline that no longer sits where it did. Most of those steps have been measured across the Pacific, and this is me drawing what the measurements say. One step has not been measured, and it is the one that decides how much of the ocean’s energy reaches a doorway.

The sawtooth riding on the trend is ENSO, and it is worth shading in rather than leaving a reader to trip over it. El Niño years lift regional sea-surface temperature by several tenths of a degree and La Niña years pull it back; 2016 and 2024 are both El Niño peaks. Without those bands the same series looks noisier than it is.

The ocean takes up roughly 91% of the excess energy in the climate system (IPCC AR6 WGI Ch. 9). That is where the cascade splits in two, and the next two sections follow one branch each.

A regional signal, built from national series too coarse to read one at a time

The SPC sea-level file reports to 0.1 metres, so every observation in it is one of five values: −0.2, −0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. That is the resolution of the file, not a property of the places in it. Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, and I would rather say that plainly than draw a flat line and let it look like a finding.

The same trend means different things depending on what sits underneath it

Rainfall anomaly is not moving one way across the Pacific. Fitting a linear trend to each of the … national series in RAIN_ANOM (…) splits the region almost evenly: … territories trending drier, … wetter. On its own that is close to a coin toss, and it would be easy to stop there.

What caught my attention is which ones. The four steepest drying trends are …. Three of those four are low atoll states, where fresh water sits as a thin lens above seawater beneath the sand and is recharged by rain and by nothing else. There are no rivers to fall back on, no lakes, no highland catchment. Identical millimetres mean something quite different there.

… are trending wetter, and I would not read that as the good half of the split. Drainage, cropping calendars and catchment sizing are all built around a distribution, and moving in either direction moves away from what they were built for.

One more thing worth putting beside the trend rather than instead of it: Nauru’s year-to-year standard deviation is … mm, close to three times New Caledonia’s … mm. The line runs downward and the year-to-year swing around it is the widest in the region, so the average year is a weaker guide there than almost anywhere else on this page.

Two measured things, and no line drawn between them

An urban-rural distance like that turns up across the region, so it reads to me as a feature of dispersed geography rather than of any one country.

The widest gap in the file is …’s, at … points on national coverage of …%. Vanuatu is at …, Fiji at …. … territories report lower coverage in 2022 than in 2000: …. Reported definitions and survey methods shift over two decades, so I would treat those three as something to look into rather than as a verdict on anyone.

Rainfall is the recharge term for an atoll’s freshwater lens, and in several of these places it is the only one. That is the mechanism, and it is exactly why I have drawn this link dashed instead of solid. The rainfall trend is measured. The water coverage is measured. Nothing in either dataset connects one to the other, and I am not going to draw a line the data has not earned.

Two million transects, no model

Digital Earth Pacific’s rates-of-change layer fits a trend to every shoreline transect across the region and reports metres gained or lost per year. … of them fall inside one of the twenty-two Pacific territories here, and … of those carry Digital Earth Pacific’s own good quality flag. Those are the only ones I have counted, using their threshold rather than one of mine. Red retreats, blue builds.

The pattern is not uniform retreat, and the literature agrees. Duvat’s 2019 synthesis of 709 islands and Kench’s 2018 work on Tuvalu both find most atoll islands stable or gaining area as reef-derived sediment moves around. Area and habitability are not the same measurement, though, and I want to be careful not to let one stand in for the other. An island can gain area on its ocean side and lose the village on its lagoon side in the same decade, and this layer would record that as a gain.

So this section measures an outcome. It does not divide that outcome between the links above, and neither will I.

The cascade is measured everywhere except where it matters most

The heat is in the record: the ten warmest years, every one of them this century.
The sea anomalies are in the record, marking a Pacific that keeps getting less settled.
The rise is in the record once the noise is averaged away, at … millimetres a year.
The rainfall shift is in the record, uneven, and steepest where there is least freshwater storage to absorb it.
The shoreline change is in the record, traced across two million transects.
And the gas is in the record, accumulating upstream of all of it.

Between the gas and the shoreline sits a chain of measurable shifts: gas → heat → sea anomalies → sea-level rise → rainfall → shoreline.

Every solid link on this page exists because somebody decided that quantity was worth counting. Every dashed one is where I ran out of measurements, not where the connection stops.

What I cannot show you is what happens in between. That is not a gap in the Pacific. It is a gap in what has been chosen for measuring.
