What a warming ocean does on its way to land.

The Pacific Cascade

Greenhouse gas becomes heat. Heat becomes a rising sea and a rainy season that no longer keeps to its old schedule. Those become a coastline that has moved. Every one of those steps has been measured. One has not, and it is the step that decides how much of the ocean’s energy ends up at somebody’s front door.

A regional signal built out of unusable national ones

The SPC sea-level file reports at 0.1-metre resolution. Every observation in it is one of five values: −0.2, −0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. At that precision no single country’s series means much on its own. Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, not because the sea stood still.

Average across all … territories and the rounding error cancels out. The dataset cannot tell you which island is worst affected. It can tell you whether the Pacific is rising, and it says yes.

Two million transects, no model

Digital Earth Pacific’s rates-of-change layer fits a trend to each shoreline transect across the region and reports metres gained or lost per year. … of them fall inside one of the twenty-two Pacific territories mapped here, and … of those carry Digital Earth Pacific’s own good quality flag. Those are the only ones counted below. Red retreats, blue builds.

The Pacific pattern is not uniform retreat, and the literature agrees. Duvat’s 2019 synthesis of 709 islands and Kench’s 2018 work on Tuvalu both find most atoll islands stable or growing in area as reef-derived sediment gets redistributed. Land area and habitability are different things. An island can gain area on its ocean side and lose the village on its lagoon side in the same decade.

This layer measures the outcome. It does not divide that outcome up between the links above.

The cascade is measured everywhere except where it matters most

The heat is in the record: the ten warmest years, every one of them this century.
The sea anomalies are in the record, marking a Pacific that keeps getting less settled.
The rise is in the record once the noise is averaged away, at … millimetres a year.
The rainfall shift is in the record, uneven, and sharpest where there was least freshwater storage to begin with.
The shoreline change is in the record, traced across two million transects.
And the gas is in the record, piling up upstream of every one of these changes.

Between the gas and the shoreline sits a chain of measurable shifts: gas → heat → sea anomalies → sea-level rise → rainfall → shoreline.

Every solid link in this cascade exists because somebody decided that quantity was worth counting.

The open question is what happens in between the measurements, and what stays invisible because we have not yet decided how to measure it.
