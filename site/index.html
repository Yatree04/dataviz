The Pacific Cascade

What a warming ocean does on its way to land. Greenhouse gas becomes heat. Heat becomes a rising sea and a rearranged rainy season. Those become a coastline that no longer sits where it did. Each of those steps has been measured. One has not and it is the step that decides how much of the ocean’s energy reaches a doorway.

A regional signal built from unusable national ones

The SPC sea-level file reports at 0.1-metre resolution. Every observation in it is one of five values: −0.2, −0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. At that precision no single country’s series means anything — Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, not because the sea stood still.

Averaged across all — territories, the quantisation error cancels. It can answer whether the Pacific is rising, and it says yes.

Ten million transects, no model

Digital Earth Pacific’s rates-of-change layer fits a trend to each shoreline transect across the region and reports metres gained or lost per year. Ten point four million of them. Red retreats, blue builds.

The Pacific pattern is not uniform retreat, and the literature agrees: Duvat’s 2019 synthesis of 709 islands and Kench’s 2018 work on Tuvalu both find most atoll islands stable or growing in area as reef-derived sediment redistributes. Land area and habitability are different things. An island can gain area on its ocean side and lose the village on its lagoon side in the same decade.

This layer measures the outcome. It does not apportion it between the links above.

The cascade is measured everywhere except where it matters most

The heat is in the record: the ten warmest years, all within this century.
The sea anomalies are in the record, marking a Pacific that is becoming increasingly unstable.
The rise is in the record, once the noise is averaged away — — millimetres a year.
The rainfall shift is in the record, uneven and sharpest where freshwater storage is already limited.
The shoreline change is in the record, traced across millions of transects.
And the gas is in the record — accumulating upstream of every one of these changes.

Between the gas and the shoreline sits a chain of measurable shifts: gas → heat → sea anomalies → sea-level rise → rainfall → shoreline.

Every solid link in this cascade was built by someone deciding the quantity was worth counting.

The question is what happens between the measurements — and what remains invisible because we have not yet decided how to measure it.
