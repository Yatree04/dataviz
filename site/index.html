The Pacific Cascade

What a warming ocean does on its way to land. Greenhouse gas becomes heat. Heat becomes a rising sea and a rearranged rainy season. Those become a coastline that no longer sits where it did. Each of those steps has been measured. One has not and it is the step that decides how much of the ocean's energy reaches a doorway.

A regional signal built from unusable national ones

The SPC sea-level file reports at 0.1-metre resolution. Every observation in it is one of five values: -0.2, -0.1, 0.0, 0.1 or 0.2 metres. At that precision no single country's series means anything — Kiribati reads 0.0 for its first three years because the true value rounded to zero, not because the sea stood still. Averaged across all … territories, the quantisation error cancels. The regional mean rises at … mm per year across …, within the range satellite altimetry independently reports for the tropical Pacific. The dataset cannot answer which island is worst affected. It can answer whether the Pacific is rising, and it says yes.

Where the rain stopped arriving, the water did not

The SDG 6.1.1 series tracks the share of each population using a safely managed drinking-water service. Kiribati — one of the four fastest-drying territories in the rainfall data — moved from 60.9% in 2000 to 75.7% in 2022. A quarter of the country still lacks it, and the national figure conceals the more useful one: Kiribati's urban population runs 28.3 percentage points ahead of its rural population.

Rainfall is the recharge term for an atoll's freshwater lens. In several of these countries it is the only one. That is the mechanism, and it is why this link is drawn dashed: the rainfall decline is measured and the water-access shortfall is measured, but nothing in either dataset attributes one to the other.

The cascade is measured everywhere except where it matters most
The heat is in the record: the ten warmest years, all within this century.
The sea anomalies are in the record, marking a Pacific that is becoming increasingly unstable.
The rise is in the record, once the noise is averaged away — 4.3 millimetres a year.
The rainfall shift is in the record, uneven and sharpest where freshwater storage is already limited.
The shoreline change is in the record, traced across millions of transects.
And the gas is in the record — accumulating upstream of every one of these changes.
Between the gas and the shoreline sits a chain of measurable shifts: gas → heat → sea anomalies → sea-level rise → rainfall → shoreline.
Every solid link in this cascade was built by someone deciding the quantity was worth counting.
The question is what happens between the measurements — and what remains invisible because we have not yet decided how to measure it.
